Your business has a you problem.

You already know video is how clients find, vet, and choose businesses like yours. You’ve known it for years. So tell me, why isn’t it happening?

Every version of “doing content” you’ve been offered requires the one resource you don’t have: you. Your face, your hours, your willingness to perform on camera between client meetings, forever.

The exit problem nobody talks about

A business built on the founder’s face is worth less the day you step back.

There’s a quieter cost, and it compounds every year you wait.

In Estate of Paul Mitchell, the Tax Court applied a 10% key-person discount, a $15 million reduction, because the company depended on one individual.1 In Furman v. Commissioner, the same discount was applied.2 It’s settled law.

The market reality is harsher: 70% of businesses that go to market never sell, and owner dependency is one of the leading deal killers.3 Meanwhile, 73% of business owners plan to exit within the next decade. That is $14 trillion in business value about to change hands.4 Half of all business exits are involuntary: health, burnout, or a market shift you didn’t see coming.5

If your marketing depends on your face, the brand walks out the door with you. The buyer doesn’t inherit your reputation, your on-camera presence, or the trust your audience built with you. They inherit a content machine that just lost its engine.

But in Estate of Stirton Oman, the key-person discount was rejected, because the founder’s successors were already in place.6 When the face of your brand is a transferable asset instead of you, the discount disappears. That’s the play.

The more personally famous you become, the worse it gets at exit.

You were sold UGC. It will not last.

It’s not your fault. The industry’s answer to “I can’t be on camera” was UGC: rent a rotating cast of strangers, buy one-off videos, hope one hits.

Here’s what they didn’t tell you: single creatives burn out fast. Meta’s own research found conversion likelihood drops about 45% by the fourth time someone sees the same creative.7 UGC is disposable by design: a new stranger every month, zero recognition, zero compounding.

Recurring faces do the opposite. Analysed across the IPA’s effectiveness databank, campaigns built around a recurring character are 30% more likely to drive profit gains and 37% more likely to grow market share.8 Recurring characters don’t wear out. Familiarity is the mechanism that makes them work.

Refresh the content. Keep the face.

Every brand you remember has a face.

Progressive has Flo. State Farm has Jake. GEICO has a gecko with an accent. These companies spend hundreds of millions to keep one recognizable character in front of you, because a face people recognize compounds.

High-performing ads are 6× more likely to use a character as a distinctive brand asset, and characters outperform assets borrowed from wider culture, like celebrities and music.9 The Fortune 500 figured this out decades ago.

Yet nobody brought it to your industry. Until now.

And no, it can’t be an AI avatar: 83% of consumers say they’ve watched a video they suspected was AI-generated, 36% say an AI-generated video would lower their perception of the brand, and 78% say they trust videos featuring real people.10 Trust is your entire sale. That’s why the face has to be real.

The window is open. But not forever.

The creator economy is on track to approach half a trillion dollars by 2027.11 US businesses put a projected $37 billion into creator marketing in 2025, growing four times faster than the overall ad market.12 88% of consumers trust recommendations from people they know over any form of advertising.13 The shift from brand-voice to human-voice isn’t coming. It’s here.

And there’s a second market hiding in plain sight.

44.9 million people in the US speak Spanish at home, one in every seven people aged five and over.14 US Latino consumer spending is $2.5 trillion, and US Latino GDP has reached $4 trillion, the fifth-largest economy on Earth, growing more than twice as fast as the non-Latino rate.15 Yet only 5.2% of US advertising and marketing spend goes to multicultural media.16 In your city, your next clients are bilingual. Your competitors literally cannot talk to them.

So you have two options. Claim your niche’s face and your bilingual buyers, or keep waiting until you “have time to do content” while a competitor claims both. There is no third option.
